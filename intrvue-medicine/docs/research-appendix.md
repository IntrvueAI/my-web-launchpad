## 1. MEDICAL SCHOOLS COUNCIL

### 1.1 Statement on the core values and attributes needed to study medicine

- Landing page: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/latest/publications/statement-on-the-core-values-and-attributes-needed-to-study-medicine/
- PDF: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/statement-on-core-values-to-study-medicine.pdf
- Published 07.05.2018; page last modified 2025-07-04. The document self-describes as the **"2018 update"**.

**⚠️ Version flag — this matters for your product.** Despite the 2025 upload path and 2025 page-modified date, the document is still the 2018 update. It maps its values onto the **Good Medical Practice 2013** domain names — "Knowledge, Skills and Performance", "Safety and Quality", "Communication, Partnership and Teamwork", "Maintaining Trust" — which were **superseded on 30 January 2024** by the GMP 2024 domains (see §2.1). So the single most-cited MSC document in admissions coaching is internally mapped to a retired version of GMP. Your rubrics should carry the MSC attributes (still current as MSC's stated position) but should *not* reproduce its domain mapping as if it were current GMP.

### 1.2 Guidance on relevant work experience — the strongest anti-checklist material MSC publishes

- Landing: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/latest/publications/guidance-on-relevant-work-experience/
- PDF: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/relevant-experience-for-applying-to-medical-school.pdf
- Published 07.05.2017; last modified 22.06.2025.

**Verbatim:**

*What counts as relevant experience:*
- "Work experience is any activity or life experience that helps you to prepare for medical school."
- "That you have had people-focused experience of providing a service, care or help to others."
- "That you have developed some of the values, attitudes and behaviours essential to being a doctor."
- "That you have a realistic understanding of medicine and in particular the physical, organisational and emotional demands."

*What schools expect:*
- "Most medical schools do not set a minimum number of hours of work experience that applicants need."
- "Whatever type of experience you have it should have happened in the two years before you apply."

*What is actually rewarded — this is the money quote for your product:*
- **"It is what you learn about yourself; about other people and about how effective care is delivered."**
- **"What you did is only half the story: make sure you show us what attributes you demonstrated."**

*Recommended:*
- "Working with other people in a caring or service role, and in particular with people who are ill."

*Explicitly NOT rewarded:*
- **"There is little benefit in repeating the same type of work experience."**
- **"Undertaking repeated periods of direct observation of health care ('shadowing') is unlikely to be of benefit."**
- "Medical schools do not generally encourage overseas clinical work experience."

*Family caring:*
- "Some medical schools may consider evidence that you have cared for elderly, disabled or unwell friends or family."
- "Providing care as a health professional differs in several ways and so other forms of work experience are valuable."

*Overseas applicants:*
- "Work experience in the NHS is not expected for overseas students."

**🔴 Coaching claims this contradicts:** (a) the widespread "you need X weeks of hospital shadowing" — MSC says most schools set no minimum and that repeated shadowing "is unlikely to be of benefit"; (b) that a hospital placement outranks a care home or retail job — MSC's stated preference is for *caring or service roles with ill people*, and it treats paid customer-service work as legitimate; (c) that overseas medical electives strengthen an application — MSC says schools "do not generally encourage" them.

### 1.3 MSC guidance on selection and interviews

- Interviews page: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/for-students/applying-to-medical-school/interviews/
- Improving how we recruit: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/what-we-do/inspiring-future-doctors/improving-how-we-recruit/

Verbatim from the interviews page: interviews are "a chance for medical schools to get to know you. They want to see how you communicate, why you're interested in medicine and whether you have the right qualities to become a good doctor."

Four formats described: **MMIs** ("consist of a variety of different 'stations', or small interview scenarios", including "practical scenarios that involve candidates interacting with an actor" and "creative stations, such as teamwork and communication stations"); **panel interviews** ("conducted by a group of two or more interviewers", "around 20 to 30 minutes", "structured" or "semi-structured"); **assessment centres** ("can take up to an entire day"); and **online interviews** including asynchronous video.

On fairness: "A fair, open and transparent admissions process helps ensure that all applicants have an equal opportunity to demonstrate their potential, no matter their background."

**MMI design resource:** *How to run a mock MMI*, published 08.05.2019 — https://www.medschools.ac.uk/latest/publications/how-to-run-a-mock-mmi/ (PDF: https://www.medschools.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/how-to-run-a-mock-mmi.pdf). Covers eight categories of station (roleplay, ethics, communication, data interpretation, teamwork among them), aimed at "teachers, careers advisers and outreach staff supporting students from underrepresented backgrounds", with "printable resources and flexible timetables". Note it also "Encourages inclusive practice, including reasonable adjustments for students with additional needs." I could not extract the marking rubric wording from the PDF — **flagged as unverified**; worth a manual read since it is the closest thing to an official MMI scoring rubric.

**Contextual admissions:** *Indicators of good practice in contextual admissions*, 08.05.2018 — https://www.medschools.ac.uk/latest/publications/indicators-of-good-practice-in-contextual-admissions/ (PDF: .../good-practice-in-contextual-admissions.pdf). Names free school meals eligibility, care leaver status and UCAT bursary eligibility as strong indicators; recommends "a combination of individual, school and area-level data" rather than postcode alone, and that policies be "transparent".

**Selection Alliance:** co-chaired by Gordon Dent (Keele) and Simon Cork (Anglia Ruskin). Key reports: *Selecting for Excellence* (2014), *MSC Selection Alliance Report 2023* (published 08.05.2024), and *Fostering Potential* (2024, the ten-year review of Selecting for Excellence).

## 2. GMC

### 2.1 Good Medical Practice 2024 — the four domains

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/good-medical-practice-2024
**In effect 30 January 2024** for doctors; **13 December 2024** for physician associates and anaesthesia associates.

**Domain 1 — "Knowledge, skills and development"** (paras 1–15)
Being competent · Providing good clinical care · Offering remote consultations · Considering research opportunities · Maintaining, developing and improving your performance · Managing resources effectively and sustainably

**Domain 2 — "Patients, partnership and communication"** (paras 16–47)
Treating patients fairly and respecting their rights · Treating patients with kindness, courtesy and respect · Supporting patients to make decisions about treatment and care · Sharing information with patients · Communicating with those close to a patient · Caring for the whole patient · Safeguarding children and adults who are at risk of harm · Helping in emergencies · Making sure patients who pose a risk of harm to others can access appropriate care · Being open if things go wrong

**Domain 3 — "Colleagues, culture and safety"** (paras 48–80)
Treating colleagues with kindness, courtesy and respect · Contributing to a positive working and training environment · Demonstrating leadership behaviours · Contributing to continuity of care · Delegating safely and appropriately · Recording your work clearly, accurately, and legibly · Keeping patients safe · Responding to safety risks · Managing risks posed by your health

**Domain 4 — "Trust and professionalism"** (paras 81–101)
Acting with honesty and integrity · Acting with honesty and integrity in research · Maintaining professional boundaries · Communicating as a medical professional · Managing conflicts of interest · Cooperating with legal and regulatory requirements

**🔴 Major coaching error to correct.** A large amount of interview material still teaches the **2013** domains: "Knowledge, skills and performance / Safety and quality / Communication, partnership and teamwork / Maintaining trust." Those are retired. Note the substantive relocations: *safety* moved out of its own domain into Domain 3 alongside colleagues and culture; *record-keeping* moved from Domain 1 to Domain 3; a new explicit duty on **"Managing resources effectively and sustainably"** appeared in Domain 1; **"Contributing to a positive working and training environment"** and **"Demonstrating leadership behaviours"** are new in Domain 3 and are the paragraphs behind GMP 2024's much-discussed anti-bullying and civility provisions.

### 2.2 Achieving good medical practice: guidance for medical students

- https://www.gmc-uk.org/education/standards-guidance-and-curricula/guidance/student-professionalism-and-ftp/achieving-good-medical-practice
- PDF: https://www.gmc-uk.org/cdn/documents/agmp-2024-for-medical-students-english_pdf-108649541.pdf
- **2024 edition**, restructured to the GMP 2024 four domains. A parallel version exists for PA and AA students.

It applies the same four domains to students, plus sections on key concern areas and fitness-to-practise processes. Core expectation, verbatim in part: students must "demonstrate a high standard of behaviour at all times" — including **outside medical school and on social media**. Specific areas: academic integrity (plagiarism, cheating, fraud), alcohol and drugs, health conditions and "insight or management of these", professional boundaries, and raising concerns about unsafe practice.

Several schools do issue this to interview candidates. The reason it works as interview preparation is the **"at all times"** point: the guidance's distinctive claim is that professional obligations attach to the *student*, off campus and online, from day one — not to the qualified doctor. An applicant who has understood that will talk about behaviour outside the hospital; one who has only skimmed it will recite the four domains back.

### 2.3 Confidentiality

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/confidentiality
**In effect 25 April 2017**; updated 25 May 2018 (GDPR/DPA 2018) and 13 December 2024 (PA/AA regulation).

**The eight principles, verbatim:**

1. "Use the minimum necessary personal information. Use anonymised information if it is practicable to do so and if it will serve the purpose."
2. "Manage and protect information. Make sure any personal information you hold or control is effectively protected at all times against improper access, disclosure or loss."
3. "Be aware of your responsibilities. Develop and maintain an understanding of information governance that is appropriate to your role."
4. "Comply with the law. Be satisfied that you are handling personal information lawfully."
5. "Share relevant information for direct care in line with the principles in this guidance unless the patient has objected."
6. "Ask for explicit consent to disclose identifiable information about patients for purposes other than their care or local clinical audit, unless the disclosure is required by law or can be justified in the public interest."
7. "Tell patients about disclosures of personal information you make that they would not reasonably expect, or check they have received information about such disclosures, unless that is not practicable or would undermine the purpose of the disclosure. Keep a record of your decisions to disclose, or not to disclose, information."
8. "Support patients to access their information. Respect, and help patients exercise, their legal rights to be informed about how their information will be used and to have access to, or copies of, their health records."

**Public interest disclosure** (https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/confidentiality/disclosures-for-the-protection-of-patients-and-others):

- Para 64: disclosure "may be justified in the public interest if failure to do so may expose others to a risk of death or serious harm." And: "The benefits to an individual or to society of the disclosure must outweigh both the patient's and the public interest in keeping the information confidential."
- Para 63 frames the typical triggers as risks from "serious communicable diseases or serious crime."
- Para 68 sets out six balancing factors: potential harm to the patient from disclosure; potential damage to general trust in medical professionals; potential harm to others if the information is withheld; potential benefits of release; the nature of the information and the patient's views; and whether the harm could be avoided by a less intrusive route.
- Para 61: "Some laws require disclosure of patient information for purposes such as the notification of infectious diseases and the prevention of terrorism."
- Para 87: "You must disclose information if it is required by law."

**The point a thoughtful applicant should be reaching for:** the threshold is *death or serious harm to others*, the test is a *balance* not a trump card, and the default is to seek consent first and to tell the patient about the disclosure. The failure mode is an applicant who says "confidentiality can be broken if someone is at risk" without the balancing step, or who treats "public interest" as a synonym for "the police asked."

### 2.4 0–18 years: guidance for all doctors

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/0-18-years — updated 13 December 2024.

Covers assessing capacity in under-16s on Gillick principles; the position of 16–17 year olds; "Sharing information with the consent of a child or young person" and sharing without consent; child protection. On sexual health it states doctors can provide "contraceptive, abortion and STI advice and treatment, without parental knowledge or consent, to young people under 16", and includes a section on conscientious objection to providing such services. **Flagged:** I could not extract the exact paragraph wording on 16–17 year olds; verify manually before using it as a rubric line.

### 2.5 Decision making and consent

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/decision-making-and-consent
**In effect 9 November 2020**; updated 13 December 2024 and **3 August 2026** (recent — worth re-checking what the August 2026 update changed before launch).

**The seven principles, verbatim:**

1. "All patients have the right to be involved in decisions about their treatment and care and be supported to make informed decisions if they are able."
2. "Decision making is an ongoing process focused on meaningful dialogue: the exchange of relevant information specific to the individual patient."
3. "All patients have the right to be listened to, and to be given the information they need to make a decision and the time and support they need to understand it."
4. "Medical professionals must try to find out what matters to patients so they can share relevant information about the benefits and harms of proposed options and reasonable alternatives, including the option to take no action."
5. "Medical professionals must start from the presumption that all adult patients have capacity to make decisions about their treatment and care. A patient can only be judged to lack capacity to make a specific decision at a specific time, and only after assessment in line with legal requirements."
6. "The choice of treatment or care for patients who lack capacity must be of overall benefit to them, and decisions should be made in consultation with those who are close to them or advocating for them."
7. "Patients whose right to consent is affected by law should be supported to be involved in the decision-making process, and to exercise choice if possible."

**🔴 Coaching error:** material still teaching "consent must be voluntary, informed and the patient must have capacity" as *the GMC framework* is teaching the pre-2020 model. The 2020 guidance is a **dialogue** model built around *what matters to this patient* and *reasonable alternatives including doing nothing* — this is the guidance's post-*Montgomery* reorientation. Principle 4 is the one applicants most often miss and the one interviewers most reward.

### 2.6 Raising and acting on concerns about patient safety

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/raising-and-acting-on-concerns — Part 1: .../part-1-raising-a-concern

- **Para 7:** the duty bites where "patient safety or care is being compromised".
- **Para 8:** contractual gagging clauses cannot prevent raising safety concerns — void under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998.
- **Para 10 — the key one:** you are justified in raising a concern "honestly, on the basis of reasonable belief and through appropriate channels, even if you are mistaken." **You do not need proof.**
- **Paras 11–15:** follow your workplace incident-reporting procedure first; for immediate serious risk report without delay; ordinarily raise with your manager or an appropriate officer (clinical director, practice partner); if the concern involves your own partner, raise it outside the practice with the medical director or governance lead; keep a record.
- **Para 16:** escalate to a regulator such as the GMC if the responsible person is part of the problem, or if local action was inadequate.
- **Para 18:** sources of help — colleagues, the **GMC Confidential Helpline (0161 923 6399)**, defence body, royal college, or the charity Protect.

**What an interviewer is listening for:** the *escalation ladder* (local first, regulator later), and the "reasonable belief, even if mistaken" point, which is what unlocks the classic MMI station where a candidate is reluctant to report because they "can't be sure".

### 2.7 Duty of candour

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/candour---openness-and-honesty-when-things-go-wrong/the-professional-duty-of-candour

**Verbatim — the four obligations when something goes wrong. Professionals must:**
1. "tell the person (or, where appropriate, their advocate, carer or family) when something has gone wrong"
2. "apologise to the person (or, where appropriate, their advocate, carer or family)"
3. "offer an appropriate remedy or support to put matters right (if possible)"
4. "explain fully to the person (or, where appropriate, their advocate, carer or family) the short and long term effects of what has happened"

There is a companion duty to encourage a learning culture by reporting errors. **Not verified from source:** the frequently-repeated line that "an apology is not an admission of liability" — that proposition is statutory (Compensation Act 2006 s.2 in England and Wales) rather than GMC wording, and I could not confirm it appears in the GMC candour guidance. Do not attribute it to the GMC.

Note the distinction your product should teach: the **professional** duty of candour (GMC/NMC, owed to every patient, triggered by anything going wrong) sits alongside the **statutory** duty of candour on organisations (CQC Regulation 20, triggered only by defined notifiable safety incidents). Coaching material routinely conflates the two.

### 2.8 Treatment and care towards the end of life

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/treatment-and-care-towards-the-end-of-life/guidance

- **Para 2, verbatim:** "patients are 'approaching the end of life' when they are likely to die within the next 12 months." Includes advanced incurable conditions, general frailty, chronic conditions at risk of acute crisis, and sudden catastrophic events.
- **Para 10, verbatim:** "decisions concerning potentially life-prolonging treatment must not be motivated by a desire to bring about the patient's death, and must start from a presumption in favour of prolonging life." Immediately qualified: there is "no absolute obligation to prolong life irrespective of the consequences for the patient."
- **Paras 11–13:** presumption of capacity; a duty to "provide all appropriate help to maximise their ability to understand"; decisions turn on **overall benefit**, weighing benefits "against the burdens and risks".
- **Para 119, verbatim:** "If a patient is in the end stage of a disease or condition, but you judge that their death is not expected within hours or days, clinically assisted nutrition or hydration must be provided if it would be of overall benefit to them." Para 120 permits not starting or withdrawing CANH where it "would not be of overall benefit to the patient."
- The guidance's "About" page confirms it "takes account of … the law prohibiting killing (including euthanasia) and assisting suicide."

**🔴 Time-sensitive flag — assisted dying.** The **Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill** has passed the Commons and, as of the parliamentary record dated **14 May 2026**, was **at committee stage in the House of Lords**. It has **not** received Royal Assent. Assisted dying remains unlawful in England and Wales as at today's date. This will be a live interview topic in the 2026/27 cycle and the position may move during the cycle — build this as a dated, updatable field, not a hard-coded fact. Scotland's separate Assisted Dying for Terminally Ill Adults (Scotland) Bill is on its own timetable — **I did not verify its current status.**

### 2.9 Social media

**🔴 The single most outdated thing in circulating coaching material.**

- "Doctors' use of social media" (2013) was **archived on 30 January 2024**. The archived PDF is explicitly labelled as such: https://www.gmc-uk.org/-/media/gmc-site/ethical-guidance/archived-guidance/doctors-use-of-social-media.pdf
- It was replaced by **"Using social media as a medical professional"**, in effect **30 January 2024**, updated 13 December 2024: https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/using-social-media-as-a-medical-professional
- It sits alongside GMP 2024 Domain 4's paragraph heading **"Communicating as a medical professional"**.

Content: "behaving professionally, maintaining boundaries and respecting patient" confidentiality; "respecting patient confidentiality, privacy and dignity"; "being honest and trustworthy in online communications"; being "open about any interests you have that may influence the" content shared; and considering "the impact that content you share may have on patient behaviour and attitudes towards health."

**The specific dead claim to kill:** the 2013 guidance contained a much-quoted requirement that doctors who identify themselves as doctors in public online discussions should also identify themselves by name. That is **archived guidance**. Any product content asserting a current GMC duty to use your real name on social media is wrong.

### 2.10 AI and innovative technologies

https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/learning-materials/artificial-intelligence-and-innovative-technologies — framed on GMP 2024. Key points: practitioners "are responsible for the decisions they take when using new technologies like AI"; must "discuss the use of innovative technologies with patients, what other options may be available and any uncertainties and limitations"; must "recognise and work within the limits of their competence"; must "report adverse incidents involving medical devices (including software, diagnostic tests, and digital tools)"; and where technology "may put patients at risk of harm", follow *Raising and acting on concerns*. Useful for the increasingly common "should AI replace doctors?" station — the official framing is *accountability does not transfer*.

## 3. UCAS

### 3.1 The personal statement, 2026 entry onwards

**Confirmed — three structured questions, verbatim:**

1. **"Why do you want to study this course or subject?"**
2. **"How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?"**
3. **"What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?"**

**Rules:**
- **4,000 characters total across all three answers combined**, including spaces.
- **Minimum 350 characters per section.**
- The question text itself does **not** count toward the limit.
- Characters may be distributed freely between the three — there is no equal-split requirement and no individual maximum beyond the shared 4,000.

Sources: https://www.ucas.com/about-us/news-and-insights/reforming-admissions and the adviser toolkit at https://www.ucas.com/advisers/help-and-training/guides-resources-and-training/pre-application-support/personal-statements-2026-entry-onwards/personal-statement-2026-entry-classroom-resources

*(Note: the two main applicant-facing UCAS personal statement pages returned 403 to the fetch tool; the questions and limits above were confirmed from two independent UCAS pages that did resolve.)*

UCAS's stated rationale is equity — structure helps applicants without extensive essay-writing support, and gives providers consistent, comparable information.

**Product implication:** question 3 is now the explicit home of work experience and volunteering, and it demands *"and why are these experiences useful"* — the reflective clause is built into the question. Combined with MSC's "What you did is only half the story", the two official sources converge hard on reflection over inventory.

### 3.2 Key dates, 2027 entry

## 5. LEGAL AND ETHICAL REFERENCE POINTS

### 5.1 Gillick competence vs Fraser guidelines

https://learning.nspcc.org.uk/child-protection-system/gillick-competence-fraser-guidelines (last updated 5 August 2022)

**Gillick competence** is the test of whether a child under 16 has sufficient understanding and maturity to consent to treatment for themselves. Lord Scarman's test, verbatim: "it is not enough that she should understand the nature of the advice which is being given: she must also have a sufficient maturity to understand what is involved."

**Fraser guidelines** are the criteria for providing contraceptive (and now sexual health) advice and treatment to an under-16 without parental consent. The practitioner should be satisfied that:

1. the young person cannot be persuaded to inform their parents or carers, or to allow the practitioner to do so;
2. "the young person understands the advice being given";
3. their physical or mental health is likely to suffer without the advice or treatment;
4. "it is in the young person's best interests to receive the advice, treatment or both";
5. "the young person is very likely to continue having sex with or without contraceptive treatment".

**🔴 The confusion, stated precisely.** The two are not two names for the same thing, and they are not interchangeable.

- **Gillick is a test of the child.** It asks: *does this child have capacity?* It applies **broadly** — NSPCC gives therapeutic support, substance misuse counselling and living arrangements as examples of contexts where it is used.
- **Fraser is a test of the decision.** It asks: *is it justifiable for me to provide this specific intervention without involving the parents?* It applies **narrowly** — contraception, STI treatment, and termination of pregnancy for under-16s.

A young person can be Gillick competent and the Fraser criteria still not be satisfied, and the Fraser criteria are not a capacity assessment. **The single most common coaching error is teaching "Fraser guidelines" as the framework for assessing a child's competence.** That is wrong. The second most common is claiming Gillick applies only to contraception — it is the general test; the contraception-specific one is Fraser. The third is treating "Gillick competent" as a permanent status rather than decision-specific.

Both frameworks are subordinate to safeguarding: NSPCC notes that a child protection assessment is required regardless, and concerns must be reported to the relevant agencies **irrespective of the young person's wishes**. An applicant who reaches for Gillick without reaching for safeguarding has half-answered the station.

### 5.2 Mental Capacity Act 2005

**The five principles — s.1(2)–(6), verbatim as enacted** (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2005/9/section/1):

- **(2)** "A person must be assumed to have capacity unless it is established that he lacks capacity."
- **(3)** "A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless all practicable steps to help him to do so have been taken without success."
- **(4)** "A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision merely because he makes an unwise decision."
- **(5)** "An act done, or decision made, under this Act for or on behalf of a person who lacks capacity must be done, or made, in his best interests."
- **(6)** "Before the act is done, or the decision is made, regard must be had to whether the purpose for which it is needed can be as effectively achieved in a way that is less restrictive of the person's rights and freedom of action."

**The two-stage test:**

**Stage 1 — the diagnostic test, s.2(1), verbatim:** "For the purposes of this Act, a person lacks capacity in relation to a matter if at the material time he is unable to make a decision for himself in relation to the matter because of an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain." Section 2 goes on to bar findings of incapacity based on age, appearance, or assumptions from behaviour, and sets the standard of proof at the balance of probabilities.

**Stage 2 — the functional test, s.3(1):** a person is unable to make a decision if unable —
"(a) to understand the information relevant to the decision, (b) to retain that information, (c) to use or weigh that information as part of the process of making the decision, or (d) to communicate his decision".
Section 3 adds that (a) is not failed if the person can understand an explanation "given to him in a way that is appropriate to his circumstances"; that short-term retention does not defeat capacity; and that the relevant information includes the "reasonably foreseeable consequences of—(a) deciding one way or another, or (b) failing to make the decision."

**Points applicants routinely get wrong:** capacity is **decision-specific and time-specific**, never global — "he lacks capacity" is not a status; the **unwise decision** principle (s.1(4)) is the one interviewers most often probe, and candidates most often abandon under pressure; and the two stages run in that order — the impairment must be identified *and* must be the reason the functional test fails ("because of" in s.2(1) is doing causal work). Note s.1(5) uses **best interests**, whereas the GMC's consent guidance uses **overall benefit** — different vocabularies for overlapping territory, and candidates who mix them mid-answer sound unsure.

### 5.3 The four principles of biomedical ethics, and the criticisms

The four principles — **respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice** — are Beauchamp and Childress's, from *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*. Raanan Gillon's defence (*Journal of Medical Ethics* 2003;29:307, https://jme.bmj.com/content/29/5/307) argues they "can explain and justify, alone or in combination, all the substantive and universalisable claims of medical ethics", describes them as "moral nucleotides that constitute moral DNA", advocates "moral ecumenism not moral imperialism" since the principles are "compatible with a wide variety of moral theories", and defends respect for autonomy as *first among equals* because it is "a necessary component of aspects of the other three".

**The standard criticisms — and note that Gillon himself concedes most of them:**

1. **They are not a theory and give no ranking.** The framework provides no method for resolving conflicts *between* the principles when they point in opposite directions — which is precisely when ethical difficulty arises. Gillon acknowledges the approach "doesn't resolve conflicts between principles or determine their scope."
2. **The checklist charge.** Gillon directly quotes and answers the criticism, attributed to John Harris, that principlism is merely "a useful 'checklist' approach to bioethics for those new to the field." That phrase is worth having in your product: **the "checklist" objection is named in the canonical defence of the framework.** The systematic version is Clouser and Gert's "A critique of principlism" (*Journal of Medicine and Philosophy*, 1990), which argues the principles function as chapter headings or mnemonics rather than action-guiding norms. *(I was unable to fetch the Clouser and Gert paper directly — the citation is from my own knowledge and should be verified before you print it.)*
3. **Scope indeterminacy.** The principles do not tell you *to whom* they are owed — the question that decides abortion, animal research and resource allocation debates is a scope question the four principles cannot settle.
4. **Cultural specificity.** The charge that respect for autonomy encodes Western liberal individualism and travels badly. Gillon disputes this and argues the framework accommodates cultural moral diversity.
5. **Imperialism.** Alastair Campbell's worry, addressed by Gillon, that principlism crowds out virtue ethics, care ethics and casuistry.

**🎯 This is your single best anti-recitation asset.** A candidate who says "autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice" and stops has produced the exact behaviour the framework's own leading defender had to write a paper to excuse. The discriminating move at interview is not naming the four — it is showing which two are in tension in *this* scenario, and saying what would have to be true for one to give way. Design your rubrics so that naming all four scores nothing on its own.

### 5.4 Confidentiality — the current legal position

**DVLA / DVA** — https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/confidentiality---patients-fitness-to-drive-and-reporting-concerns-to-the-dvla-or-dva/patients-fitness-to-drive-and-reporting-concerns-to-the-dvla-or-dva

- **Para 3:** "The Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency (DVLA) … are legally responsible for deciding if a person is medically unfit to drive."
- **Para 4:** "The driver is legally responsible for telling the DVLA or DVA about any such condition or treatment."
- **Para 6:** the doctor must "explain this to the patient and tell them that they have a legal duty to inform the DVLA or DVA."
- **Para 9:** if the patient continues to drive, "make every reasonable effort to persuade them to stop"; then consider "whether the patient's refusal to stop driving leaves others exposed to a risk of death or serious harm"; and if so, "you should contact the DVLA or DVA promptly and disclose any relevant medical information, in confidence, to the medical adviser."
- **Para 10:** "you should try to inform the patient of your intention to disclose personal information"; consider any objection they raise; and once you have disclosed, "you should tell your patient in writing once you have done so, and make a note on the patient's record."

**🔴 Two coaching errors to fix.** First, the widespread "the doctor must report the patient to the DVLA" — **no.** The *driver* carries the legal duty; the doctor's duty is to explain that duty, to try to persuade, and only then, if others face death or serious harm, to disclose. Second, the sequence is routinely truncated: candidates jump from "patient won't stop driving" straight to "I'd call the DVLA," skipping persuasion, skipping the risk assessment, and skipping the three steps around telling the patient before and in writing afterwards. The full ladder is what scores.

**Notifiable diseases** — https://www.gov.uk/guidance/notifiable-diseases-and-how-to-report-them (last updated **19 May 2026**)

- Legal basis: Public Health (Control of Disease) Act 1984; Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010; **Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2025**.
- Report urgent cases by telephone to the local UKHSA health protection team **within 24 hours**; all cases via the online service **within 3 days**.
- **"Do not wait for laboratory confirmation of the disease"** — the duty attaches to *suspected* cases.
- **HIV is not notifiable.** This is a frequent interview trap and a frequent coaching error.
- **⚠️ Recent change, April 2025:** eight conditions were added to the registered-medical-practitioner list — MERS, influenza of zoonotic origin, **chickenpox (varicella)**, congenital syphilis, neonatal herpes, acute flaccid paralysis/myelitis, disseminated gonococcal infection, and CJD. Ten organisms were added for laboratories (including norovirus, RSV and *Candida auris*). **Hantavirus disease** was added in May 2026. Any list your product publishes that predates April 2025 is now wrong — chickenpox in particular surprises people. Source: https://ukhsa.blog.gov.uk/2025/03/11/changes-to-health-protection-notification-regulations/

The conceptual point for interviews: notifiable disease reporting and DVLA disclosure sit in **different legal categories**. Notification is a **statutory duty** — GMC Confidentiality para 87, "You must disclose information if it is required by law", no balancing required. DVLA disclosure is a **public interest** discretion under paras 63–68, requiring the balancing exercise. Candidates who treat both as "breaking confidentiality when you have to" have missed the distinction the station is testing.

### 5.5 Safeguarding — realistic escalation from a non-clinical setting

This is where interview products most often teach an answer that would be wrong in the applicant's actual life. **An applicant volunteering in a school, a care home or a youth group is not a clinician, has no clinical duty, and should not be taught a clinician's escalation path.**

**In a school or college (England):** the statutory framework is *Keeping Children Safe in Education*. The **2025 edition is in force until 31 August 2026**; the **2026 edition comes into force on 1 September 2026** — i.e. it takes effect two days from today and will be the operative version for the entire 2026/27 interview season. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/keeping-children-safe-in-education--2

The route is: report to the **Designated Safeguarding Lead** (or a deputy), immediately, following the setting's own procedures. **⚠️ Verification gap:** the 2024, 2025 and 2026 PDFs all failed to fetch (404/410), so I have **not** verified the exact Part One paragraph wording or numbering. The DSL route and the "report immediately" expectation are the well-established structure of Part One, but **do not print paragraph-numbered quotes from KCSIE without checking the 2026 PDF directly** once it is live on 1 September.

**In a care home or adult social care setting:** report to the shift lead or manager, and the organisation's safeguarding lead. Escalation beyond the organisation runs to the **local authority adult safeguarding team** under the Care Act 2014, and to the CQC. **I did not verify Care Act / CQC specifics against source in this pass** — flagged.

**What is realistic to expect of an applicant, and what interviewers actually reward:**

- **Tell someone in the setting, promptly, and name who.** "I'd speak to the designated safeguarding lead" or "I'd tell the shift manager immediately" is the correct answer. Vagueness — "I'd tell someone senior" — is the weak version.
- **Do not investigate.** A volunteer does not interview the child, does not question the alleged perpetrator, does not gather evidence.
- **Do not promise confidentiality.** The classic station has a child begin with "promise you won't tell anyone." The correct move is to say you cannot promise that, and to explain why, before they disclose.
- **Do not assume someone else has acted.** If nothing appears to happen, escalate — to the next person up, and ultimately outside the organisation (local authority children's social care, NSPCC helpline, police if a child is in immediate danger).
- **Record what you saw or heard, in the child's own words, promptly.**

**What is *not* realistic and should not be taught:** that a volunteer would make a referral to social services directly as a first step; that they should assess Gillick competence; that they should conduct a capacity assessment; that they carry a professional duty of candour; or that they should contact a parent. An applicant who reaches for GMC guidance to answer a question about their Saturday job at a care home has misread the station — and, more importantly, has demonstrated exactly the framework-first reflex your product is trying to avoid.

## 6. THE ANTI-CHECKLIST QUESTION — DIRECT ANSWER

You asked where medical schools or the GMC **explicitly** warn against checklist thinking. Honest finding:

**Explicit warnings that do exist:**

| Source | What it says | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **MSC, Guidance on relevant work experience** | "What you did is only half the story: make sure you show us what attributes you demonstrated." And: "It is what you learn about yourself; about other people and about how effective care is delivered." | **Strongest available.** An explicit official statement that the inventory does not score and the reflection does. |
| **MSC, same document** | "There is little benefit in repeating the same type of work experience"; repeated shadowing "is unlikely to be of benefit." | Explicit rejection of accumulation-as-strategy. |
| **UCAS, personal statement Q3** | "…and why are these experiences useful?" | The reflective demand is written into the question itself. |
| **Gillon, JME 2003** | Quotes and answers the criticism that principlism is merely "a useful 'checklist' approach to bioethics for those new to the field." | The checklist objection is named inside the framework's canonical defence — usable as authority. |
| **GMC, Decision making and consent, principle 2** | "Decision making is an ongoing process focused on meaningful dialogue: the exchange of relevant information specific to the individual patient." | Structurally anti-checklist: the 2020 guidance replaced a checklist model of consent with a dialogue model. Principle 4's "what matters to patients" is the same move. |
| **GMC, Raising concerns, para 10** | Justified "on the basis of reasonable belief… even if you are mistaken." | Explicitly licenses judgement under uncertainty rather than rule-following. |

**Explicit warnings that do NOT exist — do not claim otherwise:**

- The MSC core values statement contains **no** warning against rehearsed answers.
- The MSC interviews page contains **no** warning about coaching companies, rehearsed answers, or over-preparation. I checked specifically.
- **Neither the MSC nor the GMC has published any statement on interview preparation companies**, or on applicants' use of AI. The only AI warning in this entire landscape is **UCAS's**, and it is about the personal statement, not the interview.
- I could not fetch MSC's official interview-prep partner site (studyinghealthcare.ac.uk — robots.txt fetch timed out). **That site is the most likely place an official anti-rehearsal statement would sit, and it is the one gap worth closing manually.**

**The honest framing for your product:** the case against reciting frameworks is not that an official body has forbidden it. It is that the official criteria are written in the language of *demonstrated attributes* and *reflection* — MSC asks what you learned and what you demonstrated; UCAS asks why the experience was useful; the GMC's consent guidance is built on dialogue and on what matters to *this* patient; and the four principles' own defender had to rebut the checklist charge rather than deny it. A rubric built to reward recall is scoring something none of these documents ask for.

**Status: fast-moving** — the single most-changed topic since early 2025.

## 5. AI in healthcare
**Status: fast-moving**

**(a) Current position.** AI has moved from pilots to funded national rollout, with a regulatory line now drawn between administrative and clinical AI.

**(b) Recent developments.**
- **4 July 2026 — NHS England "AI acceleration"**: part of a **£10bn over three years** technology/transformation programme, projected **£41bn benefit over a decade**. Components: **AI triage in the NHS App** (200,000+ patients within 12 months; all App users by **April 2028**; trial showed **29% reduction in GP phone queue volumes**); **ambient voice technology / AI notetaking** in outpatients (NHSE estimates 9,000+ extra A&E consultations/day if scaled nationally); **Microsoft Copilot for 500,000+ NHS staff** (~2 days/month admin time saved); NHS Online virtual hospital; Single Patient Record; cyber security. Sites include St George's/Epsom & St Helier, Croydon, Kingston & Richmond; Alder Hey and Manchester University FTs scaling to 3,000+ clinicians.
- **29 July 2026 — MHRA clarified AVT regulatory status**: transcription, summarisation, letter drafting and code suggestion are **not** medical devices; AVT that supports diagnosis/treatment/prevention, or acts without clinician review, **is** a regulated medical device. Clinicians remain responsible for verifying all AI outputs.
- **April 2026 — NHS England guidance** on safe use of AI-enabled ambient scribing products; first-wave AI scribe funding directed to A&E and outpatients.
- **MHRA AI Airlock** regulatory sandbox now in **Phase 2**, with a **£3.6m funding boost over three years** (gov.uk).
- 10YP ambition: "most AI-enabled care system in the world"; all hospitals "fully AI-enabled" within the plan's lifetime.

**(d) Central tension.** Accountability sits with the clinician while the tool is procured and configured centrally — MHRA's July 2026 line makes that explicit but doesn't resolve it. Live debates: whether the summarisation/diagnosis boundary is stable in practice as products add features (a functionality change can silently alter regulatory status); training-data bias and equity of triage; deskilling as ambient documentation and triage become default; and data governance overlapping with the Palantir/FDP controversy (topic 14).

## 6. Assisted dying
**Status: fast-moving — a vote is imminent (11 September 2026)**

**(a) England & Wales.** The **Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill** (Kim Leadbeater; Lords sponsor Lord Falconer) passed Commons Third Reading **20 June 2025 (314–291)**, Lords Second Reading **12 September 2025** (some sources say 19 September — ⚠️ conflict), entered Lords Committee **14 November 2025**, and **ran out of time in the Lords before the session ended in May 2026** — it did not become law. Reported failure date **24 April 2026** is when it became clear it could not pass; the session ended with the King's Speech on 13 May 2026. Peers tabled close to **1,300 amendments**, described as a record.

**A new bill has been introduced.** **Lauren Edwards MP** (second in the Private Members' Bill ballot) reintroduced the **Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill on 17 June 2026**. **Second Reading is scheduled for Friday 11 September 2026.** Campaigners argue the **Parliament Act** would prevent the Lords blocking it a second time if the Commons passes the same bill again — this is contested (see Hansard Society briefing).

Core provisions unchanged: terminally ill, mentally competent adults with **six months or less** to live; two independent doctors; a multidisciplinary expert panel; reflection periods.

**(b) Scotland.** The **Assisted Dying for Terminally Ill Adults (Scotland) Bill** (Liam McArthur MSP) **fell at Stage 3 on 17 March 2026: 69 against, 57 for, 1 abstention.** Assisted dying is therefore **not** legal in Scotland and no bill is live.

**(c) Isle of Man.** Assisted Dying Bill passed Tynwald **25 March 2025**. Eligibility: 18+, terminally ill with ~12 months' prognosis, 5 years' IoM residency, registered with an IoM GP, two independent doctors.

**(d) Jersey.** States Assembly approved the Assisted Dying Law on **26 February 2026**; **12 months' prognosis for neurodegenerative conditions** (Parkinson's, MND); healthcare providers may administer. Awaiting Royal Assent; law expected to take effect **2027**.

**(e) Central tension.** Constitutionally: an elected chamber twice-passing versus an unelected chamber's scrutiny/filibuster — and whether Parliament Act use is legitimate for a Private Member's Bill. Clinically: conscientious objection, the reliability of six-month prognosis, capacity assessment, and whether palliative care capacity is adequate. Jurisdictionally: England/Wales, Scotland, IoM and Jersey are now genuinely divergent.

**(b) Recent development.** **24 September 2025** — one-year progress report published (gov.uk).

**(c) Numbers.**
- Target: **reduce total human antibiotic use by 5% from a 2019 baseline by 2029.**
- Year 1: **1.1 million fewer** GP antibiotic prescriptions in England (2023–24), a **3.2%** reduction, ~**£6.3m** saved annually; **hospital prescribing −1.4%**; **IV treatment days per admission −4.85%**.
- Global context: AMR associated with ~**4.95 million deaths annually** (2019); UN target to cut AMR deaths **10% by 2030**.

**(d) Central tension.** Prescribing reduction targets pull against access and against pressure to treat in a system with poor diagnostic turnaround; and the UK's subscription ("Netflix") model for new antibiotics tests whether a market can be created for drugs we intend not to use. ⚠️ No 2026 progress report verified — the two-year report may be due autumn 2026.

## 12. Mental health
**Status: evolving**

**(a) Current position.** The **Mental Health Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 18 December 2025** and is being commenced in stages; most substantive reforms are **years away**. CAMHS demand continues to outstrip capacity.

**(b) Recent developments.**
- **18 February 2026** — first commencement (SI 2026/385): Secretary of State powers to transfer detained asylum seekers to hospital; and restricted patients may be conditionally discharged into the community under conditions amounting to a deprivation of liberty (reversing *MM*).
- Remaining reforms: prison-to-hospital transfer time limit expected within **18–24 months** of enactment; most other changes ~**2 years** (pending a new Code of Practice and retraining); full implementation could take **up to 10 years**.
- **6 August 2026** — **£343m** for **159 new NHS mental health facilities**: **100 walk-in community mental health centres** (no referral needed) and **59 mental health emergency departments**. £187m community / £156m EDs; 57 new-builds, 102 conversions; first sites open **autumn 2026**, more from **March 2027**. Baseline: England currently has 6 such community centres and 23 MH emergency departments.

## 13. Genomics and screening
**Status: evolving**

**(a) Recent developments.**
- **16 July 2026 — national newborn screening for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)** announced. Heel-prick test; first labs begin **October 2026** (three months ahead of schedule); remaining six labs from **October 2027**. Supported by a **£4.1m NIHR evaluation** (Oxford-led) that will inform a formal UK NSC recommendation.
- **28 May 2026 — UK NSC recommends targeted prostate cancer screening**: men aged **45–61 with a confirmed BRCA2 variant and a family history** of breast, ovarian, pancreatic or prostate cancer, PSA every two years. **BRCA1 carriers were excluded** from the final version (present in the November 2025 draft). UK governments must now decide whether to accept. Criticism: excludes Black men and family history alone; PSA unreliability (~8 in 10 positives are false).
- **1 August 2026** — NHS inviting **500,000 men** to join a major prostate cancer research programme (TRANSFORM-type screening trial).
- **25 August 2026** — NHS rolling out **at-home HPV self-sampling kits** for cervical screening to millions of women.
- **Generation Study** (Genomics England): whole-genome sequencing of **100,000 newborns**, screening **200+ rare treatable conditions**; in active recruitment across NHS trusts. ⚠️ Current recruitment total not verified.
- 10YP commitments: sequence **150,000 adults**; **universal newborn genomic testing by end of the decade**; a genomics population health service.

**(d) Central tension.** Screening is moving from population-wide to **genomically stratified** — which improves precision but hands the NHS an equity problem (who gets tested to know they're BRCA2-positive in the first place?) plus overdiagnosis, incidental findings, insurance/employment implications, and consent for newborns who cannot consent. The prostate decision is the clean exam case: the NSC declined population screening on evidence grounds against strong campaigning pressure.

## 14. Data privacy and digital
**Status: fast-moving**

**(a) Current position.** The **NHS App** is the flagship. The **Federated Data Platform** (Palantir, **£330m contract, 2023**) is operational and controversial. A **Single Patient Record** now has a legislative vehicle.

**(b) Recent developments.**
- **13 May 2026** — Single Patient Record confirmed in the King's Speech within the **NHS Modernisation Bill**: consolidating GP, hospital, specialist and social care records, accessible via the NHS App. Rollout to **maternity and frailty patients by 2028**, wider after. NHSE weighing existing EPRs vs Palantir's FDP as the substrate.
- **11 May 2026** — NHSE plans to grant Palantir and external consultancy staff **admin-level access to identifiable patient data** on the **National Data Integration Tenant**, replacing case-by-case approvals because individual applications were "too inconvenient." An internal NHS briefing acknowledged "risk of loss of public confidence." A health minister apologised for NHSE's handling; government signalled it may consider alternatives at the contract break clause. BMA pressed for full audit trails.
- **16 April 2026** — Commons debate on the NHS Federated Data Platform.

**(c) Numbers — NHS App, July 2026 (NHS England Digital):**
- **15.4m** distinct users logged in (+26% YoY); **79.9m** login sessions; **42.2m** registered accounts (+9% YoY).
- **7.6m** repeat prescriptions ordered (+27%), by 6.1m unique users.
- **36.8m** messages delivered (+60% YoY); 41% read within 24 hours; 65% of registered users opted into notifications.
- 3,210 pharmacies (31%) offer prescription tracking; 2,211 (21%) offer "Ready to Collect."
- 10YP: NHS App to be "a full front door to the entire NHS **by 2028**."

**(d) Central tension.** Two-sided. (i) **Consent and control**: an opt-out-shaped Single Patient Record plus a US defence-linked contractor with admin access is the sharpest NHS data-trust question since care.data — the government's own briefing concedes the confidence risk. (ii) **Digital exclusion**: making the App the front door disadvantages people without smartphones, connectivity, digital skills or English — disproportionately older, poorer, and disabled people, i.e. the highest users of care. ⚠️ I could not verify current UK digital-exclusion statistics from a primary source (the gov.uk Digital Inclusion Action Plan URL 404'd) — **re-check before citing any figure**.

## 15. Private sector involvement
**Status: evolving**

**(a) Current position.** Policy is settled in favour of **routine, planned use of independent sector capacity** for NHS-funded elective, diagnostic and neurodevelopmental work — a continuation and expansion, not a reversal.

**(b) Recent developments.**
- **6 January 2025** — *Elective recovery: a partnership agreement between the NHS and the independent sector* (NHS England). Independent sector treated **over 1 million NHS patients in 2024**. Commits to shared planning, data sharing, transparent payment, patient choice, workforce sustainability, and conflict-of-interest safeguards.
- **10YP (July 2025)** — continue using private capacity "where it is available"; explore **public-private partnerships for neighbourhood health centres** (business case decision at the autumn budget); talks on expanding NHS provision in the most disadvantaged areas.
- **NHS England draft 2026/27 contract** revises elective payment rules affecting independent providers (Mills & Reeve, Jan 2026).
- **IHPN, 6 August 2026** — independent-sector diagnostics providers delivered ~**4 million NHS scans in 2025/26**, more than official data captured.
- **IHPN, 20 July 2026** — independent providers deliver **over 50% of NHS-funded ADHD assessments**.
- **IHPN, 20 August 2026** — independent providers now a significant share of NHS-funded cancer treatment.
- **IHPN, 28 August 2026** — "Going Private" report claims growing cross-political openness to private healthcare use.

**(d) Central tension.** Independent providers add throughput but draw on the **same clinical workforce**, concentrate on lower-complexity casemix (leaving trusts the complex, less remunerative work), and are unevenly distributed — so patient choice may widen inequalities rather than narrow them. Note also the reverse direction of travel in improvement policy (NHSE partnering with the independent sector on *improvement* support), and the growth of self-pay as a symptom of NHS waits.

## 16. Other unusually prominent 2026 stories

**Maternity and neonatal safety — arguably the biggest UK health story of 2026.**
- **24 June 2026** — **Ockenden final report into Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust** (HC 284, 401 pages; DHSC). ## What has changed most since early 2025

1. **The government itself.** Starmer → **Burnham (PM, 20 July 2026)**; Streeting → Murray (14 May 2026) → **Cooper (20 July 2026)**. Every ministerial attribution in older content is wrong.
2. **The resident doctors' dispute ended** (29 June 2026), after five multi-day strikes across 2025–26 — but **consultants now hold a strike mandate** (76%, July 2026). The centre of gravity has shifted from residents to consultants.
3. **A new law changed medical careers**: the **Medical Training (Prioritisation) Act 2026** (Royal Assent 5 March 2026) collapsed specialty competition ratios from ~4:1 to ~2:1 for UK graduates and cut non-prioritised applicants to under 2% of offers.
4. **Waiting lists turned a corner**: the 18-week interim target was **met in March 2026 (65.3%)** — content written in early 2025 describing "60% and falling short" is now wrong. Cancer and A&E have *not* improved comparably.
5. **NHS England's abolition acquired a bill**: the **NHS Modernisation Bill** (King's Speech, 13 May 2026), which also abolishes **Healthwatch** and merges **HSSIB into the CQC**; ICBs 42→26; "new DHSC" from April 2027.
6. **Assisted dying failed and restarted**: the Leadbeater bill died in the Lords at the end of the session (May 2026); Scotland's bill **fell at Stage 3 on 17 March 2026**; Jersey legalised it (26 Feb 2026); a **new bill (Lauren Edwards) faces Second Reading on 11 September 2026** with the Parliament Act in play.
7. **Physician associates**: the **Leng Review** (16 July 2025) reset the role — renaming, scope limits, supervision — and GMC registration becomes mandatory on **13 December 2026**.
8. **AI went national**: **£10bn/3 years**, AI triage in the NHS App, Copilot for 500,000 staff, and an **MHRA regulatory line drawn on 29 July 2026**.
9. **Maternity safety became the defining scandal**, with two landmark reports in a single week (24 and 30 June 2026) and a statutory Commissioner in prospect.
10. **Tobacco generational ban is law** (29 April 2026), with the first affected cohort turning 18 in January 2027.
11. **Health inequalities widened**: ONS's April 2026 release put the **healthy life expectancy gap at ~20 years**.
12. **Social care got a Fair Pay Agreement mechanism** (16 July 2026) but Casey's Phase 1 report is still pending, with public engagement launched 29 July 2026.
13. **The 10 Year Workforce Plan is still missing** — twice delayed, unpublished as of at least 8 July 2026.
